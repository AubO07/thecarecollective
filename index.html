People’s lives are connected.
Their support should be too.

What if the services around a person and their carer worked together—so support arrived before progress broke down?

We’re developing a practical pilot with the people who know the gaps: those receiving care, unpaid carers and the people delivering support.

Read the full proposal ↓

01 · The problem we’re solving

Families are carrying the connections.

Housing, benefits, health, social care, learning and employment can each shape someone’s recovery and independence. When decisions and handovers happen separately, people and families are left to join them up.

Chasing decisions, repeating information and repairing missed handovers takes time and energy away from caring—and from both people’s own lives. A problem left unresolved in one service can become an emergency for another.

This proposal grew from Aubrey Ghose’s experience as a carer and his participation in local co-production over the past few years.

03 · The idea

Build on the NHS. Connect the support around it.

The blue NHS line is our baseline. The question is how the wider care ecosystem can connect around it, with a shared understanding of what matters to the person and their carer.

Each person retains their own voice and choices. Participating services would agree what information can lawfully be shared, for what purpose, and who is responsible for acting on it.

Permission from people. Responsibility from services.

04 · What we want to test

Could better connections change the trajectory?

Less time repairing the system could mean more time for recovery, relationships, learning and work. The fuchsia curve illustrates that possibility; it is not a forecast or a measured result.

Two people. Distinct ambitions.

A carer might retain four days of paid work while the person they support enters education. Both might return to part-time work. Or the gain might be safer, more independent daily life.

Measure the costs separately

Compare the cost of coordination and support with changes in avoidable service use and public revenue. Track household earnings and wellbeing separately. National savings remain a question for evidence, not a promise.

05 · A practical first test

Five boroughs. One hundred family partnerships. Six months.

Start small. Learn together.

The proposed pilot involves 20 family partnerships in each borough, using existing services and an agreed coordination process.

  1. Agree each person’s goals and information-sharing choices.
  2. Build a shared picture and name who coordinates support.
  3. Act on delays and missed handovers before problems escalate.
  4. Compare outcomes and costs with a suitable baseline or comparison group.

What would count as progress?

Less time chasing services. More successful handovers. Earlier support. Sustained independence. Progress towards learning, training or work where wanted and appropriate.

AI could eventually help identify emerging patterns. First, test the basics: useful information, clear responsibility and action. Predictive claims would require separate validation.

A positive result would support a larger test. Wider use would depend on evidence across different people and places.

Connecting the Dots and my lived experiance.

My dad and I have experienced both the best and the worst of our health and care system.

We’ve seen what can happen when people work together, communicate properly and help someone move forward with their life.

Last year when things were working well and thanks in large part to Richmond Housings efforts, I was independent, going out most days, seeing friends again, meeting people and making plans for the future.

I’d had two job interviews and was looking forward to returning to work. And to contributing further with NHS and University pilots for speach and Occupational Therapy’s

And my dad was doing the same.

He’d developed The Care Collective, a website and business idea he was preparing to launch, and we were both looking forward to getting on with our lives again.

We’d reached that point because, when the different services worked together, we were able to make real progress.

But we’ve also experienced what happens when that coordination breaks down.

Progress that has taken months or years to achieve can be lost surprisingly quickly. Things that were going well can suddenly become complicated, and people find themselves having to fight to recover opportunities they’d already worked hard to regain.

What I find difficult to understand is why the different organisations involved in someone’s life can’t simply communicate better with one another.

It seems such an obvious thing to do.

My hope is that Connecting the Dots can test whether a more joined-up approach actually delivers better outcomes for people.

Not by creating another complicated system, but by making the organisations we already have work together more effectively.

And by recognising that behind every case number, address or postcode are real people, with their own lives, ambitions, relationships and futures.

A carer and the person receiving care are two individuals. Both deserve to be heard, both deserve independence, and both deserve the opportunity to get on with their lives.

We’ve experienced what happens when the system works well. We know the difference it can make.

I hope Connecting the Dots can help make that experience something other people can rely on, rather than something that depends on everything happening to fall into place.

If what we’ve been through can help others avoid similar difficulties and achieve better outcomes, then something positive will have come from some very difficult times.
